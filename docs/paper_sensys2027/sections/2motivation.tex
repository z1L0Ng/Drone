\subsection{From Convenience Interface to Safety Layer}

If drones are to coexist with people in everyday environments, then human-drone interaction cannot be framed only as a convenience feature. It must also support timely human intervention. A person near a drone should be able to interrupt it, redirect it, or de-escalate its behavior without needing expert tooling, a handset, or a perfectly functioning network connection. This is especially important when drones operate in close proximity to uninvolved people.

This motivation is consistent with both prior systems work and current regulatory direction. Existing UAV speech-interface research shows that spoken interaction is feasible in principle \cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}, while EU drone regulations explicitly frame UAS deployment in terms of safety, security, privacy, data protection, and operational responsibility \cite{eu2019uasops,eu2019uasdesign}. More broadly, the EU AI Act emphasizes human-centric and trustworthy AI with a high level of protection of health and safety \cite{eu2024aiact}. These sources do not imply that voice is the only answer, but they do support the need for interaction mechanisms that preserve human oversight in realistic deployment settings.

Under this view, the paper is not mainly about adding speech recognition to a drone. It is about designing a lightweight \emph{voice safety layer}: a system component that lets nearby humans naturally express high-priority intents and lets the drone respond conservatively when the signal is uncertain.

\subsection{Why Small Drones Are the Hardest First Instantiation}

Small drones are a strong target for this idea because they combine close-proximity operation with severe acoustic self-interference. The same vehicle that should remain interruptible near people also generates rotor noise close to its microphones. That noise is broadband, non-stationary, and coupled to propulsion state, which makes it fundamentally different from background office noise or household ambience. Prior UAV-focused studies have already shown that voice-command recognition deteriorates in the presence of hovering drones and challenging operating environments \cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

This matters for paper positioning. The manuscript should stay centered on drones, because drones are already a sufficiently challenging and important setting on their own. A brief future-looking point is still reasonable: if the interaction abstraction works well for drones, similar ideas may later matter for other nearby robots. But that is outlook, not the subject of this paper.

\subsection{Why Intent-Level Modeling Is the Right Initial Scope}

Once the problem is framed as a safety layer, transcript-first speech processing becomes a weak default. The system does not need to recover arbitrary language with perfect lexical fidelity. It needs to recognize a small number of operationally meaningful states with low latency and conservative failure behavior. Spoken language understanding has increasingly moved toward direct speech-to-intent modeling, showing that useful intent decisions can be learned without full transcripts \cite{lugosch19_interspeech,kuo20_interspeech,mhiri20_interspeech}.

That direction is a better match for our setting for three reasons. First, onboard execution is easier to justify when the output space is narrow. Second, the \emph{unknown} class can be elevated to a first-class safety mechanism rather than treated as leftover error. Third, the evaluation target becomes more honest: instead of asking whether the system can transcribe speech in rotor noise, we ask whether it can reliably identify when a human is trying to stop, direct, or disengage from a nearby drone.

This is why the current paper adopts a narrow abstraction of \emph{emergency}, \emph{movement}, and \emph{unknown}. That taxonomy is not the end state of human-robot conversation. It is the minimal interface that makes the safety-layer idea experimentally testable.

\subsection{Why the Evaluation Should Not Be Tied to One Airframe}

The real-world setup should also reflect this drone-centered framing. A Tello-class drone is a useful engineering starting point, but a paper built entirely around one airframe risks telling the wrong story. The stronger claim is that a common voice-safety abstraction can be instantiated across drone platforms, even if each platform introduces different microphones, propulsion signatures, and controller interfaces.

For that reason, the evaluation should separate \emph{what is airframe-specific} from \emph{what should transfer across drones}. Airframe-specific aspects include rotor spectrum, microphone mounting, and control APIs. More stable aspects include the safety-oriented command abstraction, the need for an explicit rejection path, and the requirement that human interruption remain possible under self-noise. This separation will make the paper more robust as the prototype expands beyond a single drone.

\subsection{Scope of This Draft}

This draft does not claim that the full future-world problem is solved. Its narrower purpose is to establish a credible first systems path for drones: define the safety-layer abstraction, instantiate it on self-noisy drone platforms, and evaluate whether the resulting voice interface remains usable under realistic acoustic interference. The manuscript should therefore use the broader motivation only to justify the problem choice, while keeping its main empirical claims grounded in the current drone-based evidence.
