\section{Introduction}
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs/drones) are expected to become more prevalent in our daily lives, crisscrossing human life. Drones are increasingly expected to operate alongside people for inspections, assistance, education, shows, transportation, parcel delivery, and, importantly, hazardous tasks and other low-altitude tasks. UAVs may also independently plan their tasks, missions, etc., and become more autonomous as we advance in the application of artificial intelligence.   In these settings, interaction is not only a convenience problem. Voice commands, e.g., asking a drone to move, hold position, stop a task, or respond to a safety-critical situation without opening an app or reaching for a controller, will become the most important interaction.

Given the potential extent of applications of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), policymakers have been contemplating ensuring the deployment of these systems is bound by safety standards and regulations to potentially reduce unwarranted consequences. For example, the \textit{European Aviation Safety Agency} in Regulation 2019/947 has provided guidelines for the widespread deployment of UAVs, particularly in urban areas. The guidelines instruct UAV operators to perform a risk assessment and mitigation before UAVs are considered airworthy, and may require UAVs to implement fail-safe systems. Most available UAVs on the market lack fault-detection systems, which can pose a safety risk. Soon, we expect regulators worldwide to establish safe operating conditions.


Speaking to the drone is a natural interaction mode because it fits how people already communicate in shared spaces. Voice command is attractive for this role, especially in emergency situations, because it is natural, immediate, and directed toward the drone. When the drone is very close --or about to crash into--people or structures, the drone should understand voice commands, since that is the natural way humans react. However, various sounds in the surroundings, including propeller noise, create a messy acoustic environment, making it difficult to recognize speech commands. This issue is compounded when a small UAV/quadrotor drone needs to recognize voice commands because the microphone is very close to the propellers, which induces self-noise, and the noise spectrum changes with motor speed, distance, and the drone's motion. Prior work has shown that UAV speech interfaces are possible in principle~\cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}, while UAV-noise studies show that drone operating conditions can degrade voice-command discerning capabilities~\cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

This paper argues that the right systems abstraction is not a general speech recognizer mounted on a drone. The useful abstraction is an onboard \emph{UAV safety interaction layer}, a local component that lets people interact with the drone while keeping the control interface narrow, auditable, and conservative. The component does not turn arbitrary speech into flight commands. It narrows recognition to a small intent-state contract, keeps emergency as a safety-critical state, and reserves unknown for audio that should not influence control.

This abstraction changes what must be designed and what must be evaluated. A conventional command recognizer can be judged by whether it selects the correct term spoken.  A talk-to-the-drone interface also needs a control to implement  safety mechanisms, such as how a safety-critical emergency intent is represented (True positive) versus what prevents a coarse usual other utterances becoming an unintended flight command (false positive). The paper, therefore, treats the speach recognition and safety command execution as a single system. How good the recognition is also matters, but it is only one part of the evidence needed for a voice-driven UAV safety mechanism.

\textbf{Our Approach.} The design follows three principles. First, intent recognition should be learned directly from short audio frames rather than through a \textit{transcript-first} pipeline. For a nearby drone, the important question is whether speech expresses a small set of actionable interaction and not every word need to be recovered. Second, embedded inference is part of the problem: the interaction layer should remain local to the vehicle, meaning the recognizer must fit the operator and memory constraints of the microcontroller. Third, any connection to control must be logged and mediated. A classifier score is not a safety mechanism; the recognizer becomes useful only when its output is converted into a stateful event that can be held, rejected, escalated, or forwarded according to the control policy.

We build this idea as a simple local pipeline. The drone listens through an onboard microphone and turns each short segment of audio into a voice event rather than a flight command. A movement event means the person may be asking for an ordinary action, but the control policy still decides whether anything should happen. An emergency event means the speech should be treated as safety-critical and brought to the front of the interaction logic. An unknown event means the audio is unsupported or uncertain, so it should not change the drone's behavior. Our prototype implements the local listening and recognition path on a small ESP32 board and sends these event records to a bridge that logs and mediates possible actions.

The evidence follows the same layered structure. Offline tests show whether the recognizer can learn the voice-event categories under rotor-noisy audio. Board tests show whether the drone-side hardware can capture audio, run the recognizer, and report events locally. Control-side tests must then show how those events are logged, ignored, escalated, or forwarded by the bridge. The current draft separates these layers because recognition accuracy alone does not prove a safe UAV interaction, and a working board pipeline does not prove that the full system is ready for live flight. The evaluation section therefore reports each layer on its own terms and leaves full-system validation as explicit work to complete before submission.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Safety Interaction Layer.} We define a drone-side UAV safety interaction layer that treats human voice as a safety-relevant signal under rotor self-noise, runs locally, and exposes events only through a conservative control boundary rather than a general speech assistant or direct speech-to-flight controller.
\item \textbf{Intent-State Modeling.} We model voice input as safety-relevant states and events rather than merely three labels: emergency maps to safety-critical handling, movement maps to pending ordinary interaction that requires additional policy before motion, and unknown maps to fallback/no-action behavior.
\item \textbf{Rotor-Noise Robust On-Device Recognition.} We instantiate the recognizer path on XIAO ESP32-S3/TFLM with microphone capture, feature extraction, int8 inference, and event reporting, positioning the board as the edge deployment path for rotor-noisy safety interaction without claiming live flight or fully onboard drone control.
\item \textbf{Evaluation and Reproducibility Plan.} We structure the evaluation around recognition quality, rotor-noise robustness, deployment feasibility, speaker and language robustness protocols, and an artifact-release plan for reproducing the safety interaction pipeline; claims in these dimensions are reported only when the corresponding result files and artifacts are available.
\end{itemize}
