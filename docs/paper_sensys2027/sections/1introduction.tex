\section{Introduction}
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs/drones) are expected to become more prevalent in our daily lives, crisscrossing human life. Drones are increasingly expected to operate alongside people for inspections, assistance, education, shows, transportation, parcel delivery, and, importantly, hazardous tasks and other low-altitude tasks. UAVs may also independently plan their tasks, missions, etc., and become more autonomous as we advance in the application of artificial intelligence. As UAVs operate closer to people and take on more autonomous low-altitude tasks, interaction becomes more than a convenience feature: nearby people need a way to communicate with UAVs without always relying on a controller, phone application, or network connection. Voice commands, e.g., asking a drone to move, hold position, stop a task, or respond to a safety-critical situation without opening an app or reaching for a controller, can become an important interaction channel in these settings.

Figure~\ref{fig:talk_to_drone_scenario} illustrates the interaction style this paper targets: nearby speech becomes an input to a UAV-side safety interaction layer rather than a direct replacement for the existing control stack.

Given the potential extent of applications of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), policymakers have been contemplating ensuring the deployment of these systems is bound by safety standards and regulations to potentially reduce unwarranted consequences. For example, the \textit{European Aviation Safety Agency} in Regulation 2019/947 has provided guidelines for the widespread deployment of UAVs, particularly in urban areas. The guidelines instruct UAV operators to perform a risk assessment and mitigation before UAVs are considered airworthy, and may require UAVs to implement fail-safe systems. Most available UAVs on the market lack fault-detection systems, which can pose a safety risk. Soon, we expect regulators worldwide to establish safe operating conditions. 

Speaking to a drone is a natural interaction mode because it aligns with how people already communicate in shared spaces. For a nearby UAV, voice is especially useful because it is hands-free: a person can express intent without reaching for a controller, opening a phone application, or relying on a network connection. When the drone is very close to people or structures, or is about to collide with them, it should be able to understand voice commands, since speech is often the most natural way humans react in such situations.

However, the acoustic environment around a UAV is often highly challenging, with propeller noise and other surrounding sounds making speech recognition difficult. This problem becomes more severe for small UAVs or quadrotors, where the microphone is close to the propellers, causing strong self-noise. In addition, the noise spectrum changes with motor speed, distance, and drone motion. Prior work has shown that UAV speech interfaces are feasible in principle~\cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}, while studies on UAV noise indicate that operating conditions can significantly degrade voice-command recognition~\cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

A straightforward response is to attach a conventional speech interface to the drone: transcribe the audio and parse the text, detect keywords, or map a recognised command directly to an action. These approaches are attractive because they are familiar, but they leave a systems gap for UAV safety interaction. Short rotor-noisy clips can make transcript-first parsing brittle, keyword or command labels do not define how speech should be mediated before physical action, and direct mapping can turn recognition errors into action pressure.

This paper introduces \emph{Akouo}, an onboard voice safety layer for small UAVs. Akouo listens for nearby speech and converts each short audio window into a structured safety-state event before any action is considered. The event-state contract is deliberately small: movement represents an ordinary control-oriented request, emergency represents safety-critical speech, and unknown contains unsupported or uncertain audio. Akouo keeps this contract narrow so that ambiguous speech can be logged and contained instead of being forced into a flight command.

This framing changes the evaluation question. Recognition accuracy still matters, but the system must also show why the event-state contract is needed, whether the recognizer can sustain the contract under rotor noise, whether the embedded path can produce events locally, and whether the safety-state boundary prevents unsupported speech from becoming an unintended action. We therefore evaluate Akouo against transcript-first, compact-classifier, and direct-mapping alternatives, not only as an isolated speech classifier.

\textbf{Our Approach.} Akouo follows three principles. First, the system should focus on the safety-relevant intent of speech, not on recovering every word. Second, the voice layer should run locally on the UAV whenever possible, so the onboard recognizer must fit the computation, memory, and operator constraints of the embedded processor. Third, speech should not directly trigger drone motion. The system must first create a structured safety-state event, record confidence and timing, and pass the event through a safety-state boundary before any vehicle-facing response is allowed.

The prototype shows how this listening and recognition path can run close to the drone and produce structured safety-state events for a boundary that logs and mediates possible actions.

Akouo is not a replacement for collision avoidance, geofencing, emergency landing, or manual override. Instead, it adds a way for nearby people to express speech-based intent, which the UAV can record and check before any action is considered.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Safety-State Mediated Voice Interaction.} We define Akouo, a UAV-side safety interaction layer that treats nearby speech as a safety-relevant signal under rotor self-noise and admits it through a safety-state boundary rather than as a general speech assistant or direct speech-to-flight controller.
\item \textbf{Event-State Contract.} Akouo models voice input as structured safety-state events rather than merely command labels: emergency maps to safety-critical handling, movement remains pending until policy supplies authority and context, and unknown provides containment for unsupported or uncertain audio.
\item \textbf{Rotor-Noise Robust On-Device Recognition.} We instantiate the event source on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller with microphone capture, feature extraction, inference, and event reporting, positioning the board as the edge deployment path for rotor-noisy safety interaction without claiming live flight or fully onboard drone control.
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} We evaluate recognition quality, rotor-noise robustness, embedded deployment feasibility, safety-state boundary behavior, and participant-level use while contrasting Akouo with transcript-first ASR, compact speech-command classifiers, direct mapping, and no-unknown ablations.
\end{itemize}
