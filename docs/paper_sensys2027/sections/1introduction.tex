\section{Introduction}

Small drones are increasingly expected to operate near people during inspection, assistance, education, campus demonstrations, and other low-altitude tasks. In these settings, interaction is not only a convenience problem. A nearby person may need a simple way to interrupt a drone when the vehicle is drifting toward a person, continuing after a task has changed, or operating in a way that makes bystanders uncomfortable. The interruption path should not assume that the person is holding the right handset, that the drone still has a reliable network link, or that the operator can reach a controller in time.

Voice is attractive for this role because it is already available to people around the drone. A short emergency utterance can be produced without reaching for an interface, and it can be directed toward the vehicle rather than toward a remote service. However, the same drone that needs to hear the interruption also creates the acoustic condition that makes it difficult. A small UAV places the microphone close to rotor self-noise, and the noise spectrum changes with motor state, distance, and vehicle motion. Prior work has shown that UAV speech interfaces are possible in principle \cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}, while UAV-noise studies show that drone operating conditions can degrade voice-command discrimination \cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

This paper argues that the right systems abstraction is not a general speech recognizer mounted on a drone. The useful abstraction is an onboard \emph{voice safety component}: a local component that listens for emergency interruption intent under rotor noise and emits an auditable safety event to the control stack. The component does not turn arbitrary speech into flight commands. It narrows recognition to a small contract, treats emergency as the primary event, and reserves a separate unknown outcome for audio that should not influence control. This keeps the recognizer useful for interruption while avoiding the stronger and less defensible claim that all spoken commands can be safely executed.

This abstraction changes what must be designed and evaluated. A conventional command recognizer can be judged by whether it selects the right phrase from a menu. An emergency safety component also needs a control-facing contract: what happens when confidence is low, what state is logged when an emergency event appears, and what prevents a coarse movement utterance from becoming an unintended flight command. The paper therefore treats the recognizer and bridge as a single systems path. Recognition quality matters, but it is only one part of the evidence needed for a safety component.

The design follows three principles. First, emergency interruption should be recognized directly from short audio windows rather than through a transcript-first pipeline. For a nearby drone, the important question is whether the audio contains a high-priority interrupt event, not whether every word can be recovered. Second, embedded inference is part of the problem: the interruption path should remain local to the vehicle, which means the recognizer must fit the operator and memory constraints of the microcontroller path. Third, any connection to control must be logged and mediated. A classifier score is not a safety mechanism; the safety component becomes useful only when its output is converted into a stateful event that can be held, rejected, escalated, or forwarded according to the control policy.

We instantiate this design with a noise-aware recognizer and an ESP32/TFLM prototype. The recognizer maps log-mel features to three outcomes: emergency, movement, and unknown. Emergency is the central event for the paper. Movement remains an auxiliary class that separates ordinary control-like speech from high-priority interruption, and unknown is the guardrail for unsupported or low-confidence audio. The prototype runs local microphone capture, feature extraction, integer inference, and USB CDC event reporting on a XIAO ESP32-S3, then exposes the resulting event fields to a conservative control bridge.

The paper is intentionally conservative about evidence. Offline noisy-set results support the emergency intent recognizer. ESP32 runtime logs support the local event path. Dry-run and gate artifacts inform the bridge design. None of these by themselves establish labeled real-world semantic accuracy, no-propeller bridge behavior, or live-flight safety. The evaluation section keeps these boundaries explicit so the paper can grow toward a submission without hiding the remaining validation work.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item It frames nearby-drone voice interaction as an emergency interruption component for small UAVs under rotor self-noise, rather than as open-ended ASR or direct speech-to-flight control.
\item It defines a narrow intent contract in which emergency is the primary safety event, movement is an auxiliary intent, and unknown protects the control interface from unsupported audio.
\item It designs a noise-aware recognizer family with a model-quality anchor and an embedded student that preserves the same safety-event contract under ESP32/TFLM constraints.
\item It presents an embedded prototype and evaluation plan that separates offline noisy-set recognition, board-side runtime feasibility, and the still-needed control-bridge validation.
\end{itemize}
