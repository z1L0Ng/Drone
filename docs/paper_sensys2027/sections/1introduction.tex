\section{Introduction}

In the future environments we care about, small drones will not remain isolated research devices. They will move closer to people during inspection, delivery, assistance, and routine everyday tasks. In that world, interaction is not only a convenience problem. It is also a safety problem: people need a lightweight, socially natural way to interrupt, redirect, or calm a nearby drone without reaching for a controller, opening an app, or relying on a perfect network connection.

Voice is a strong candidate for that role because it is immediate, hands-free, and already embedded in human behavior. But for small drones, voice is also unusually hard to use well. Prior work has shown that speech interfaces for UAV control are feasible, including direct command-recognition systems and multimodal extensions that combine speech with visual context \cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}. The difficulty is that the drone most in need of a vocal safety channel is often the drone that destroys the acoustic conditions needed for speech understanding: a small drone injects strong rotor noise near the microphone, and that self-noise is broadband, non-stationary, and entangled with short spoken commands. UAV-specific studies have already documented substantial degradation in voice-command recognition under hovering-drone and adverse operating conditions \cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

This future-facing motivation is also consistent with the regulatory direction around autonomous and semi-autonomous systems. In the European Union, the operational rules for unmanned aircraft systems explicitly frame drone deployment around risks to safety, privacy, personal data protection, security, and the environment, while the corresponding product-side regulation defines design and manufacturing requirements for UAS placed on the market \cite{eu2019uasops,eu2019uasdesign}. More broadly, the EU AI Act states that the purpose of the regulation is to promote human-centric and trustworthy AI while ensuring a high level of protection of health and safety \cite{eu2024aiact}. We do not claim that these regulations prescribe a vocal safety interface. Rather, they reinforce the underlying systems argument: drones operating near people need interaction mechanisms that preserve human oversight and safe intervention under realistic deployment constraints.

We therefore argue that the right framing is not ``speech interface for one specific drone,'' and not ``general ASR on a flying platform.'' The more important systems problem is how to build a \emph{voice-triggered safety layer} for small nearby drones. Such a layer should be able to recognize a limited set of safety-relevant spoken intents, reject uncertain audio, and remain usable under the drone's own self-noise. A Tello-class drone can be one prototype instantiation, but it should not define the full scope of the paper because the problem should generalize across drone platforms. In the longer term, similar design ideas may also matter for other nearby robots, but this paper is specifically about drones.

This perspective shifts the modeling goal. In spoken language understanding, a growing body of work has moved from transcript-first pipelines toward direct speech-to-intent modeling, showing that useful intent decisions can be learned without requiring full word-level transcription \cite{lugosch19_interspeech,kuo20_interspeech,mhiri20_interspeech}. That direction is especially appealing here because the system does not need to understand arbitrary language; it needs to capture a small number of high-value interaction states such as \emph{emergency stop}, \emph{movement request}, and \emph{reject/unknown}. What matters most is not transcription fidelity but whether the drone remains gracefully interruptible under realistic deployment constraints: onboard execution, low latency, bounded false triggers, and resilience to strong self-noise.

This paper takes a first step toward that broader goal. We adopt a deliberately narrow command abstraction of \emph{emergency}, \emph{movement}, and \emph{unknown}, and study how to make that abstraction reliable enough to serve as a practical safety mechanism for small drones operating near people. Our current implementation centers on an on-device audio-to-intent pipeline with class-aware augmentation and clean-to-noisy teacher-student transfer, drawing inspiration from robust speech-training methods such as Noisy Student while adapting them to self-noisy drone settings \cite{park2020improvednoisystudent}. The paper's point is therefore not that a single drone platform has been solved, but that this safety-oriented framing leads to a clearer system architecture and a more defensible evaluation target for drone interaction.

Under this framing, the real-world setup is intentionally broader than a single airframe. A Tello-based prototype can serve as one concrete development platform, but the paper should treat the real-world study as a \emph{drone safety-interface evaluation} that may span multiple drone types as the implementation matures. That choice makes the story stronger: the contribution is the design of a voice safety mechanism for human-drone co-presence, with current experiments serving as the first instantiation rather than the full boundary of the problem.

This paper therefore makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate voice interaction for small drones as a \emph{safety-layer problem}: the system should keep the drone interruptible and socially usable under severe self-noise, rather than optimizing only generic speech accuracy.
\item We instantiate this idea as an on-device, emergency-aware audio-to-intent pipeline with a narrow command abstraction and a training design centered on noise robustness, rejection of uncertain audio, and clean-to-noisy transfer.
\item We argue for a drone-centered evaluation methodology that combines benchmark robustness with real-world safety-interface tests across multiple drone platforms, instead of binding the paper narrative to a single Tello-style prototype.
\end{itemize}
