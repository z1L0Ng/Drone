\section{Introduction}
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs/drones) are expected to become more prevalent in our daily lives, crisscrossing human life. Drones are increasingly expected to operate alongside people for inspections, assistance, education, shows, transportation, parcel delivery, and, importantly, hazardous tasks and other low-altitude tasks. UAVs may also independently plan their tasks, missions, etc., and become more autonomous as we advance in the application of artificial intelligence. As UAVs operate closer to people and take on more autonomous low-altitude tasks, interaction becomes more than a convenience feature: nearby people need a way to communicate with UAVs without always relying on a controller, phone application, or network connection. Voice commands, e.g., asking a drone to move, hold position, stop a task, or respond to a safety-critical situation without opening an app or reaching for a controller, can become an important interaction channel in these settings.

Given the potential extent of applications of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), policymakers have been contemplating ensuring the deployment of these systems is bound by safety standards and regulations to potentially reduce unwarranted consequences. For example, the \textit{European Aviation Safety Agency} in Regulation 2019/947 has provided guidelines for the widespread deployment of UAVs, particularly in urban areas. The guidelines instruct UAV operators to perform a risk assessment and mitigation before UAVs are considered airworthy, and may require UAVs to implement fail-safe systems. Most available UAVs on the market lack fault-detection systems, which can pose a safety risk. Soon, we expect regulators worldwide to establish safe operating conditions. 

 
Speaking to the drone is a natural interaction mode because it fits how people already communicate in shared spaces. For a nearby UAV, voice is especially useful because it is hands-free: a person can express intent without reaching for a controller, opening a phone application, or relying on a network path. When the drone is very close --or about to crash into--people or structures, the drone should understand voice commands, since that is the natural way humans react. However, various sounds in the surroundings, including propeller noise, create a messy acoustic environment, making it difficult to recognize speech commands. This issue is compounded when a small UAV/quadrotor drone needs to recognize voice commands because the microphone is very close to the propellers, which induces self-noise, and the noise spectrum changes with motor speed, distance, and the drone's motion. Prior work has shown that UAV speech interfaces are possible in principle~\cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}, while UAV-noise studies show that drone operating conditions can degrade voice-command discerning capabilities~\cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

This paper introduces \emph{Akouo}, an onboard voice safety layer for small UAVs. The layer listens for nearby speech, maps it into a small set of intent events before any action is taken. In this paper, an intent is the system's interpretation of what the speaker is trying to communicate: an ordinary movement request, a safety-critical emergency request, or unsupported speech. Akouo keeps this set deliberately small so that ambiguous audio can be logged and rejected instead of being forced into a flight command.

This framing changes the evaluation question. Recognition accuracy still matters, but the system must also show that emergency speech is treated as safety-critical, ordinary movement speech remains subject to downstream policy, and unsupported or uncertain audio does not become an unintended command. We therefore evaluate Akouo as both a recognizer and a control workflow.

\textbf{Our Approach.} Akouo follows three principles. First, the system should focus on what the speaker is trying to communicate, not on recovering every word. Second, the voice layer should run locally on the UAV whenever possible, so the onboard recognizer must fit the computation, memory, and operator constraints of the embedded processor. Third, speech should not directly trigger drone motion. The system must first record what was heard, how confident the recognizer was, and what decision was made before any action is allowed.

The prototype shows how this listening and recognition path can run close to the drone and produce event records for a bridge that logs and mediates possible actions.

Akouo is not a replacement for collision avoidance, geofencing, emergency landing, or manual override. Instead, it adds a way for nearby people to express speech-based intent, which the UAV can record and check before any action is considered.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Safety Interaction Layer.} We define Akouo, a drone-side UAV safety interaction layer that treats human voice as a safety-relevant signal under rotor self-noise, runs locally, and exposes events only through a conservative control boundary rather than a general speech assistant or direct speech-to-flight controller.
\item \textbf{Intent-State Modeling.} Akouo models voice input as safety-relevant states and events rather than merely three labels: emergency maps to safety-critical handling, movement maps to pending ordinary interaction that requires additional policy before motion, and unknown maps to fallback/no-action behavior.
\item \textbf{Rotor-Noise Robust On-Device Recognition.} We instantiate the recognizer path on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller with microphone capture, feature extraction, inference, and event reporting, positioning the board as the edge deployment path for rotor-noisy safety interaction without claiming live flight or fully onboard drone control.
\item \textbf{Evaluation and Reproducibility Plan.} We structure the evaluation around recognition quality, rotor-noise robustness, deployment feasibility, speaker and language robustness.
\end{itemize}
