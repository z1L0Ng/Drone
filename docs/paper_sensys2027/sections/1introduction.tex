\section{Introduction}

Small drones are increasingly expected to operate near people during inspection, assistance, education, campus demonstrations, and other low-altitude tasks. In these settings, interaction is not only a convenience problem. A nearby person may want to ask a drone to move, hold position, stop a task, or respond to a safety-critical situation without opening an app or reaching for a controller. Speaking to the drone is a natural interaction mode because it fits how people already communicate in shared spaces.

Voice is attractive for this role because it can be hands-free, immediate, and directed toward the vehicle rather than toward a remote service. However, the same drone that should understand nearby speech also creates the acoustic condition that makes speech difficult. A small UAV places the microphone close to rotor self-noise, and the noise spectrum changes with motor state, distance, and vehicle motion. Prior work has shown that UAV speech interfaces are possible in principle \cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}, while UAV-noise studies show that drone operating conditions can degrade voice-command discrimination \cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

This paper argues that the right systems abstraction is not a general speech recognizer mounted on a drone. The useful abstraction is a \emph{safety-state mediated voice interaction architecture}: a local component that lets people talk to the drone while keeping the control interface narrow, auditable, and conservative. The component does not turn arbitrary speech into flight commands. It first converts speech into constrained intent/state events, treats emergency as a safety-critical state, treats movement as pending interaction rather than motion, and reserves unknown/fallback for audio that should not influence control.

This abstraction changes what must be designed and evaluated. A conventional command recognizer can be judged by whether it selects the right phrase from a menu. A talk-to-the-drone interface also needs a control-facing safety mechanism: what happens when confidence is low, how a safety-critical emergency intent is represented, and what prevents a coarse movement utterance from becoming an unintended flight command. The paper therefore treats the recognizer, event contract, and bridge as one systems path. Recognition quality matters, but it is only one part of the evidence needed for a voice-driven UAV safety mechanism.

The design follows three principles. First, intent recognition should be learned directly from short audio windows rather than through a transcript-first pipeline. For a nearby drone, the important question is whether speech expresses a small set of actionable interaction states, not whether every word can be recovered. Second, embedded inference is part of the problem: the interaction layer should remain local to the vehicle, which means the recognizer must fit the operator and memory constraints of the microcontroller path. Third, any connection to control must be logged and mediated. A classifier score is not a safety mechanism; the recognizer becomes useful only when its output is converted into a stateful event that can be held, rejected, escalated, or forwarded according to the control policy.

We instantiate this design with a rotor-noise-centered recognizer and an ESP32/TFLM prototype. The recognizer maps log-mel features into safety-relevant states: movement becomes a pending ordinary interaction that still requires policy, emergency becomes a safety-critical event, and unknown/fallback becomes no-action behavior for unsupported or low-confidence audio. The prototype runs local microphone capture, feature extraction, integer inference, and USB CDC event reporting on a XIAO ESP32-S3, then exposes the resulting event fields to a conservative control bridge.

The paper is intentionally conservative about evidence. Offline noisy-set results support the intent recognizer. ESP32 runtime logs support the local event path. Dry-run and gate artifacts inform the bridge design. None of these by themselves establish labeled real-world semantic accuracy, no-propeller bridge behavior, or live-flight safety. The evaluation section keeps these boundaries explicit.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Safety-mediated voice interaction for UAVs.} We introduce a drone-side interaction architecture in which human speech is treated as a safety-relevant local signal under rotor self-noise and is exposed to control only through constrained, auditable events.
\item \textbf{Intent-to-state abstraction instead of command classification.} We define an event contract that maps emergency to safety-critical handling, movement to pending ordinary interaction that requires downstream policy, and unknown/fallback to no-action behavior for unsupported or low-confidence audio.
\item \textbf{Rotor-noise-centered recognition design.} We build the recognizer around drone self-noise rather than clean-speech or generic command conditions, using a reproducible noisy-set anchor to evaluate whether the event contract can be recognized under rotor-contaminated audio.
\item \textbf{Deployment-constrained safety event generation.} We separate the model-quality anchor from the embedded deployment candidate and instantiate local microphone capture, feature extraction, int8 TFLM inference, and event reporting on XIAO ESP32-S3 as a constrained event source for the conservative bridge.
\end{itemize}
