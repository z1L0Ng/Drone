\section{Introduction}

Voice is an attractive control channel for small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) because it supports hands-free interaction during navigation, inspection, and emergency handling. Prior work has already shown that speech interfaces for UAV control are feasible, both in direct command-recognition settings and in multimodal extensions that combine speech with visual context \cite{oneata19_interspeech,oneata2021multimodal}. The problem is that the operating condition most in need of voice assistance is also the one that degrades speech most severely: the UAV itself injects strong rotor noise near the microphone, and that noise is broadband, non-stationary, and acoustically entangled with short spoken commands. Measurements from prior UAV-specific studies confirm that voice-command recognition degrades substantially in the presence of hovering drones and adverse operating conditions \cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise,park2023uavvehicle}.

These constraints make our target setting different from conventional speech benchmarks in two ways. First, the system must run fully onboard, without assuming cloud connectivity, remote ASR, or external microphone infrastructure. Second, aggregate accuracy is not a sufficient objective because the highest-cost errors are concentrated on a small set of intents such as \emph{stop}, \emph{hold}, and \emph{land}. In other words, a practical UAV voice interface is not just a speech model; it is part of a safety-relevant control loop whose usefulness depends on low latency, bounded false triggers, and reliable recovery under heavy self-noise.

This motivates a shift in problem formulation. In spoken language understanding, a growing line of work has moved from transcript-first pipelines toward direct speech-to-intent modeling, showing that strong intent decisions can be learned without requiring full word-level transcription \cite{lugosch19_interspeech,kuo20_interspeech,mhiri20_interspeech}. That direction is especially appealing for UAVs, where command vocabularies are narrow and transcription errors can cascade into unsafe actions. However, most prior end-to-end SLU work targets assistants or generic command domains rather than rotor-dominated, on-device UAV operation. The missing piece is not just intent classification under noise; it is a deployment-oriented design that explicitly prioritizes emergency reliability and rejects uncertain audio before it reaches the flight controller.

This paper therefore adopts a deliberately narrow command abstraction: \emph{emergency}, \emph{movement}, and \emph{unknown}. That scope is more defensible for an initial SenSys submission than claiming open-vocabulary speech understanding under extreme rotor noise. It also aligns with the strongest evidence currently available in our repository, where the best completed results come from direct audio-to-intent modeling with noise-aware training rather than from transcript-first pipelines. Specifically, we start from a direct noisy-training baseline and then add class-aware augmentation plus clean-to-noisy teacher-student transfer, drawing inspiration from robust speech-training methods such as Noisy Student while adapting them to intent recognition under UAV self-noise \cite{park2020improvednoisystudent}.

The resulting gap is already large enough to justify the paper's main story. On the current balanced noisy benchmark at evaluation SNR $=-10$ dB, the direct noisy baseline reaches only $0.72$ overall accuracy and $0.47$ emergency recall. Our best completed configuration improves these numbers to $0.88$ overall accuracy and $0.79$ emergency recall. These gains matter because they move the model closer to the operational requirement that emergency intents remain recognizable even when the drone itself is the dominant acoustic interferer.

This paper therefore makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate rotor-noisy UAV voice control as an \emph{on-device intent-recognition} problem with an emergency-aware command abstraction, instead of relying on full transcription under severe acoustic interference.
\item We present a robust training pipeline that combines noisy mixing, class-aware augmentation, and clean-to-noisy teacher-student transfer, and we show that it substantially improves emergency reliability on the current noisy benchmark.
\item We structure the evaluation around deployment-relevant evidence: benchmark robustness first, then prototype integration, onboard latency, false-trigger behavior, and real-world transfer as explicit validation gates.
\end{itemize}
