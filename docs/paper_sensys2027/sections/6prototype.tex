\section{Prototype}
\label{sec:prototype}

\subsection{Embedded Intent-State Source}

The prototype turns the architecture into a local intent-state source near the UAV. An ESP32-S3 microphone board captures audio, forms one-second analysis windows, computes log-mel features, invokes the embedded integer recognizer, and reports the resulting intent-state update to a safety-state boundary implementation. The board is not the flight controller. Its responsibility is to make the voice layer local: speech can be heard and summarized near the drone before control-side policy considers action.

This placement is intentionally close to the acoustic source. If the microphone and recognizer depend on a phone, laptop, or remote service, the voice channel inherits the availability and latency of that external path. By putting capture and inference on the microcontroller, the prototype tests the harder system question: whether the UAV-side device can produce an intent-state update under the same resource limits that would constrain an onboard safety accessory.

The embedded path is organized around steady intent-state generation. Audio capture and model execution are separated so that the system can keep accepting new windows while processing the previous one. This split matters because a one-second voice interface depends not only on the cost of a single model invocation, but also on whether the prototype can repeatedly produce one update per audio window. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports both the invocation time and the observed intent-state stream timing.

The dual-core organization follows that goal. One side of the prototype is responsible for acquiring the next audio window at a stable cadence. The other side owns feature extraction, integer inference, output formatting, and intent-state reporting. This keeps microphone capture from being blocked by the recognizer whenever possible. It also makes timing easier to interpret: capture time, frontend time, model invocation, intent-state reporting, and boundary-handling delay can be recorded as separate quantities rather than collapsed into a single opaque response-time number.

The update sent by the board contains the predicted intent state, confidence, timing fields, and diagnostic output needed to interpret the decision. These fields are intentionally small. The board does not send a high-level drone command such as takeoff, land, or move. It sends the evidence required for the safety-state boundary to decide what to do next.

\subsection{Intent-State Reporting and Safety-State Boundary}

The prototype reports intent-state updates to a lightweight bridge component that implements the safety-state boundary for the laboratory system. In this section, \emph{bridge} refers only to that implementation component, not to a separate paper abstraction. The boundary implementation attaches the current interaction state, applies the safety-state policy, and records the outcome. This arrangement keeps the embedded board focused on local speech processing while making the control decision inspectable.

The intent-state payload is deliberately closer to a sensor report than to a command. It includes a sequence number or timestamp, the predicted intent state, confidence, timing information, and status fields. The boundary implementation adds the state that the board should not decide locally: whether manual authority is present, whether the vehicle is allowed to respond, whether a prior update is still active, and whether a vehicle-facing response completed or timed out. This split lets the prototype test the safety interaction layer without requiring the recognizer to understand the full UAV policy.

The prototype evidence path follows the paper's safety-interaction story: nearby speech is captured locally, converted into an intent-state update, recorded for inspection, and then mediated before any vehicle-facing action can occur. Local sensing and onboard inference produce the update; the boundary implementation combines that update with policy, authority, and vehicle state before deciding whether to hold, reject, or forward it.

The safety-state boundary exposes the same semantics as the architecture. Emergency updates are routed to safety-critical handling under policy. Movement updates remain pending until the boundary decides whether the request is authorized and safe. Unknown updates are logged and mapped to no action, and the reported confidence field is available for future threshold policies. This behavior lets the prototype exercise the intent-state mapping without treating the recognizer as a direct controller.

The boundary implementation also supports repeatable inspection of failure cases. If the board reports an unknown update, the boundary record shows whether it was ignored. If a movement update is produced while manual authority is absent, the record shows whether the update was blocked. If an emergency update is produced, the record shows whether it entered the intended safety-critical path. These records explain the mediated response shown in the emergency demonstration because they expose the step that a speech benchmark cannot measure.

\subsection{Emergency Response Boundary}

The control side is implemented as a mediated emergency-response boundary for the laboratory demonstration in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. During the demonstration, the UAV continues normal operation while the embedded intent-state source keeps processing local audio windows. When emergency speech is detected, the safety-state boundary treats the update as a safety-critical request and routes it to a configured safe landing or stopped response. Speech still does not become an arbitrary movement command; it becomes an intent-state update that the boundary interprets under policy.

This setup is useful because it exposes the part of the system that a speech-only benchmark cannot see. A recognizer may correctly label a window as emergency, but the paper's claim depends on whether that update reaches a mediated response path while the drone is operating. The demonstration therefore connects local inference, intent-state generation, boundary handling, and visible UAV behavior in one controlled scene.

A flight-facing version of \sys{} can move more of the safety-state boundary logic onto the vehicle or into a tightly integrated companion process. The current prototype is a step toward that design: it demonstrates local intent-state generation, mediated emergency response, and visible safe landing or stopped behavior while preserving a clear barrier between speech recognition and physical actuation.

This boundary is also what makes the demonstration more informative than a model label alone. The viewer can see the person speaking, the embedded recognizer continuing to run, the emergency update entering the safety-state boundary, and the UAV moving to a safe landing or stopped state. The result is evidence that the proposed interaction loop works in a constrained laboratory scene, while broader safety validation remains a separate requirement.
