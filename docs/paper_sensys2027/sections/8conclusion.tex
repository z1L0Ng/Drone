\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\sys{} argues for a simple shift in how voice should be used around small UAVs. Speaking to a drone is natural and hands-free, but speech should not be treated as direct flight control. The safer abstraction is a voice interaction layer that listens locally, converts nearby speech into constrained intent events, and hands those events to a conservative boundary before the control stack can respond.

This layer complements existing UAV safety mechanisms. Obstacle avoidance monitors nearby objects, geofencing monitors position, failsafe landing monitors vehicle health, and manual override gives authority to an operator. \sys{} adds a human speech channel that can express safety-critical and ordinary interaction intent under rotor self-noise. Its contribution is not that voice replaces those mechanisms, but that voice becomes a structured input they can reason about.

The design also changes how voice interaction should be evaluated for UAVs. A speech-only benchmark can show whether the recognizer selected the right event, but it cannot show whether that event was safely handled. A hardware benchmark can show whether the embedded device produced events at the required cadence, but it cannot show whether those events were meaningful. A demonstration can make the interaction visible, especially when interpreted alongside event records and component measurements. \sys{} ties these pieces together by making the event record the common object across recognition, runtime, bridge behavior, and user interaction.

This framing is useful beyond the specific prototype. Many future UAV interfaces will combine human input, onboard perception, and autonomous policy. The lesson from \sys{} is that natural input should enter such systems through a narrow and auditable boundary. For voice, that means recognizing intent without pretending to understand every utterance, exposing uncertainty rather than hiding it, and making the control-side response visible. The same principle can guide other human-facing signals that are useful near a UAV but too ambiguous to execute directly.

The design also clarifies how evidence should be separated. Recognition quality matters, especially for emergency recall and unknown containment. The rotor-noise SNR sweep supports the acoustic robustness claim, but it remains separate from control-boundary or flight evidence. Embedded runtime matters because the voice layer operates near the drone rather than depending on a remote service. Control-boundary behavior matters because a correct label is not enough unless the event is handled safely. User-facing evidence matters because the system is ultimately meant for people speaking near a UAV, not only for offline audio files.

The embedded recognizer also shows why the bridge is part of the safety argument rather than an implementation detail. Its single-window emergency recall is 0.70 in Table~\ref{tab:offline_recognition_results}, lower than the offline acoustic reference model. \sys{} does not hide this gap by treating one embedded prediction as the whole decision. The deployed path runs continuously, reports one event opportunity per audio window in steady state, and exposes confidence and timing to the bridge. The bridge can then use temporal policy, repeated evidence, confidence thresholds, and vehicle state to decide whether an emergency-like event should be escalated, held, or ignored. This bridge compensation does not replace better recognition, and it should be quantified with the final participant and demonstration logs; it explains why the paper evaluates the voice layer as a continuous event system instead of a one-shot classifier.

Physical acoustic design remains a deployment question rather than a completed evaluation result. A future onboard accessory may use enclosure design, microphone placement, or acoustic shielding to reduce rotor self-noise before the recognizer sees the signal. The current paper treats those choices as design variables for later platform integration, not as measured evidence in the evaluation section.

The main limitations are the same boundaries that make the system testable. The embedded recognizer has lower emergency recall than the offline acoustic reference model. The controlled laboratory emergency demonstration supports end-to-end feasibility, but broader flight-facing safety evaluation is still needed before making general deployment claims. Participant-level trials, keyword coverage, physical acoustic design, and live rotor-noise studies determine how broadly the interaction works outside the initial acoustic setting. These are not separate afterthoughts; they are the next measurements required by the architecture.

Overall, \sys{} frames talk-to-the-drone interaction as an additional UAV safety interaction layer. By keeping speech local, reducing it to auditable intent events, and mediating those events before control, the design offers a path toward natural voice interaction without turning arbitrary speech into direct motion.
