\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Beyond the Current Evaluation}

The evaluation establishes the main system contrast and points to natural extensions for deeper analysis. The current baseline set already separates three alternatives: transcript-first parsing, compact speech-command classification, and direct action mapping. Future comparisons can make this contrast more explicitly boundary-aware. For example, ASR baselines can be compared under the same one-second rotor-noisy window constraint while varying parser strictness, command vocabulary, and transcript confidence to show whether failures come from missing transcripts, wrong words, or overly permissive parsing. Compact classifiers can be compared not only by accuracy and emergency recall, but also by how much unsupported audio they push toward actionable classes when wrapped by the same boundary rules.

A natural extension is an operating-point curve for the safety-state boundary. The current recognition and ablation tables use argmax predictions, while the system design exposes confidence as part of the intent-state update. Sweeping confidence thresholds would report emergency recall against unknown false-action rate and directly connect recognition quality to the safety-layer claim: the question is not only which model gets the most labels right, but which recognizer and boundary setting best preserve emergency sensitivity while containing unsupported speech.

The participant study can also be extended without turning the paper into a usability study. The current live onboard capture-to-inference trials expose participant-level variation and the gap between open-source test-split speech and newly collected prototype speech. Additional reporting could separate keyword-level errors, participant-level calibration, and repeated-trial stability. These measurements would help explain whether the embedded recall loss comes from the model, the recording path, speaker variation, or specific emergency phrases.

\subsection{Extending the Safety-State Interface}

\sys{} currently uses a deliberately small set of intent states because the first goal is safe speech admission, not full conversational control. The natural next step is to expand the state space while preserving the same design discipline. Additional states such as hold, land, resume, cancel, or request-attention could make the interface more useful, but each new state should carry explicit boundary semantics: what the voice layer is allowed to report, what checks are required before action, and what fallback behavior applies when confidence is low.

Movement is the most important example. In the current design, movement is intentionally broad; it records that the speaker may be asking for ordinary UAV interaction, but it does not encode a complete navigation target. A future version could decompose movement into safer intermediate states, such as directional intent, distance-qualified intent, or follow/return intent. That expansion should not bypass the safety-state boundary. Instead, it should make the boundary more precise by separating what the recognizer heard from what the UAV is authorized to do.

The safety-state boundary itself also benefits from more quantitative treatment. Here, the boundary is evaluated through action-pressure simulations, embedded timing, and a controlled emergency demonstration. Future work can define boundary-level metrics such as admitted-action rate, rejected-unknown rate, emergency escalation latency, false emergency interruption rate, and recovery behavior after a rejected update. These metrics would let the paper compare boundary configurations rather than only recognizer architectures.

\subsection{Is Akouo a Pluggable UAV Safety Layer?}

The evidence supports \sys{} as a pluggable safety interaction layer at the system-interface level. Akouo does not require speech to replace the flight controller, obstacle avoidance, geofencing, failsafe landing, or manual override. It adds a separate voice-facing path that listens near the UAV, converts speech into constrained intent states, and exposes those states to boundary handling before any UAV-facing response. This separation is what makes the layer pluggable: the recognizer can improve, the boundary rules can change, and the downstream vehicle stack can remain conservative.

The current measurements support each part of that insertion point. Akouo-Ref and Akouo-Edge show that emergency, movement, and unknown are separable under the fixed rotor-noisy comparison condition. The embedded timing shows that the local onboard path can sustain one update opportunity per one-second window after startup. The ablations show why the unknown state is not cosmetic: removing fallback or binding labels directly to action increases action pressure from unsupported audio. The controlled demonstration shows the intended interaction loop in a visible UAV scenario, and the participant trials show that the embedded recognizer can be exercised through live onboard capture on newly collected speech.

This is still not a full validation of UAV safety. The paper does not claim that the layer proves collision avoidance, replaces certified failsafes, or covers every speech condition. Its claim is narrower and more system-specific: for small UAVs operating near people, speech should be admitted through a constrained intent-state layer, and \sys{} demonstrates that this layer can be implemented locally, evaluated against naive speech-interface alternatives, and connected to a conservative safety-state boundary without treating speech as an immediate command.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\sys{} frames talk-to-the-drone interaction as a safety-state mediated way to admit nearby speech into small UAV operation. Instead of treating speech as a transcript, keyword trigger, or direct command, Akouo converts short rotor-noisy windows into emergency, movement, or unknown intent states and passes them through a safety-state boundary. Akouo-Ref reaches 86.2\% accuracy and Akouo-Edge reaches 84.8\% accuracy under the fixed -10 dB comparison point; the embedded path sustains a steady one-second onboard update cadence; and 1462 live participant trials show 0.764 aggregate accuracy. These results do not prove UAV safety by themselves; they support the systems argument that safer speech admission begins with constrained intent-state updates rather than direct speech-to-action binding.
