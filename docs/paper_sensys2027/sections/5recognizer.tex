\subsection{Task and Dataset Construction}

The recognizer is the acoustic part of the voice safety layer. Its input is a one-second audio window captured near the drone. Its output is an intent-state update for the architecture in Section~\ref{sec:system}: emergency, movement, or unknown. The recognizer does not produce a transcript, does not parse open-ended language, and does not select flight commands. It only decides which safety-relevant intent state the current audio window should report.

This mapping matches the physical setting. Small UAVs create their own acoustic interference, and the microphone can be close to the propellers. The recognizer therefore sees speech mixed with structured rotor self-noise, motor-speed changes, and environmental background audio. A transcript-first pipeline would focus on recovering words that the safety-state boundary is not allowed to execute directly. \sys{} instead asks whether the captured audio should be treated as safety-critical speech, ordinary movement interaction, or unsupported information.

The acoustic dataset follows the same three-intent mapping. The speech examples are organized from Google Speech Commands and mapped into the three intent categories used by \sys{} \cite{warden2018speechcommands}. Emergency maps to words such as stop, no, and related reaction words; movement maps to directional and navigation words such as go, up, down, left, right, follow, and back; unknown maps to remaining in-vocabulary words and background audio segments. This organization lets the recognizer learn the safety-layer distinction directly: urgent speech, ordinary control-oriented speech, and audio that should stay on the no-action path.

The processed speech set is balanced before training. After scanning the three target classes and downsampling to the smallest class, the dataset contains 50,040 one-second utterances, with 16,680 examples per class. The split is stratified into 30,024 training examples, 10,008 validation examples, and 10,008 test examples; each validation and test split contains 3,336 examples per intent class.

Rotor noise is added from a local bank of 231 one-second recordings sampled at 16 kHz from a Tello quadrotor. The recordings were captured with the same ESP32 microphone path used by the prototype and cover operating phases such as takeoff, hovering, and landing. To choose the primary noise level for the Speech Commands mixtures, we used matched calibration recordings from the same environment and speaker: the speaker produced the same utterances once in a quiet condition and once while the Tello was running. This comparison gave an approximate full-window waveform SNR of \(-10 \pm 2\) dB for the local capture setup, not an acoustic SPL measurement, so the fixed rotor-noisy condition used in the main recognition comparisons is centered at \(-10\) dB. For each clean speech window \(x\) and noise window \(n\), the mixer computes speech power \(P_x\) and noise power \(P_n\), chooses a target noise power
\[P_n^\star = \frac{P_x}{10^{\mathrm{SNR}_{dB}/10}},\]
and forms the mixture \(y = x + \alpha n\), where \(\alpha = \sqrt{P_n^\star/P_n}\). If the mixed waveform exceeds full scale, the whole waveform is peak-normalized. This SNR-controlled construction lets the evaluation vary rotor-noise severity while keeping the intent labels, split, and recognizer fixed.

\subsection{Model Design}

Figure~\ref{fig:recognizer_architecture} gives the block structure; the purpose of the design is to turn a short, rotor-noisy audio window into one intent-state update. The recognizer is not asked to recover a word sequence. It only needs enough acoustic evidence to decide whether the window sounds like emergency speech, ordinary movement speech, or unsupported audio. This is why the input is a compact log-mel view rather than a transcript: each one-second waveform becomes a fixed \(256 \times 32 \times 1\) tensor, with 256 mel bands and 32 time frames. The frequency axis preserves rotor harmonics and speech energy; the time axis preserves the short temporal cues that distinguish words such as stop, no, go, and follow.

The first part of the recognizer reduces the spectrogram into a sequence that is easier to reason over. A small convolutional frontend compresses the mel axis while keeping the one-second time axis intact. This avoids spending the temporal encoder on a high-resolution spectrogram image, but it also avoids collapsing the window too early. After the convolutional stem, each time step carries a compact acoustic summary that can still reflect both speech cues and rotor-noise structure.

The temporal encoder then combines local and window-level evidence. It follows the Branchformer-style idea of pairing a local acoustic path with a global attention path for speech representation \cite{peng2022branchformer}. The local branch is meant to capture short speech and rotor-noise cues, such as abrupt stops, syllable onsets, and narrowband motor structure. The global branch aggregates context across the full one-second window so that the recognizer can use the whole utterance rather than isolated frames. For embedded deployment, the local path uses gated pointwise projections around a depthwise temporal convolution instead of the heavier cgMLP-style local module used in the original Branchformer. This choice keeps the useful local/global split while making the temporal path easier to export through the ESP32/TFLM inference stack. The pooled output is a single embedding because the downstream system expects one intent-state update per audio window, not a sequence of recognized words.

Akouo-Ref and Akouo-Edge use this same recognizer idea for two different roles. Akouo-Ref is the stronger offline recognizer used to characterize the acoustic design. Akouo-Edge keeps the same input and output meaning but uses the embedded version of the network: it adds a compact bottleneck before temporal encoding and uses a depthwise temporal convolution path that can be converted to the ESP32/TFLM inference path. The difference is not a different task or a different safety abstraction. Both recognizers emit the same three intent states; Akouo-Edge simply uses the version that can be carried into the embedded prototype.

The final layer emits an intent-state label and a confidence score. The label gives the safety-state boundary a constrained interpretation of the audio window. The confidence score exposes uncertainty without letting the model decide what action should follow. In the current prototype, live onboard inference reports this confidence field with each intent-state update; the recognition tables in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} use argmax predictions to keep the offline comparisons fixed.

\subsection{Deployment-Oriented Training and Optimization}

Training uses a paired clean/noisy view of the same utterance. For each recognizer version, the clean-view network and the noisy-view network have the same architecture and size. The clean-view network sees the unmodified speech example. The noisy-view network sees the same speech mixed with rotor noise. This pairing is important because the system does not want the noisy recognizer to learn a different task; it wants the same intent representation to remain stable after rotor self-noise is added.

The clean-view network is trained or reused first and then frozen. During noisy training, the noisy-view network receives the rotor-noisy feature tensor while the frozen clean-view network receives the matching clean tensor. The loss aligns the normalized embedding of the noisy view with the embedding of the clean view. In other words, the training procedure encourages the model to place clean and noisy versions of the same utterance near each other in the intent space, while still preserving the distinction among emergency, movement, and unknown.

This training process is used for both Akouo-Ref and Akouo-Edge. In the evaluation, these names refer to the resulting noisy-view recognizers, not to the clean-view networks. Akouo-Ref is produced from the stronger offline architecture, while Akouo-Edge is produced from the embedded architecture. Each recognizer has its own clean/noisy training pair. This separation keeps the paper's comparison clear: Akouo-Ref shows the acoustic behavior available with the stronger recognizer, while Akouo-Edge shows what remains when the same idea is constrained to the embedded inference path.

The training design follows the system mapping. Emergency speech needs to remain detectable when the drone is loud, movement speech needs to stay separate from emergency, and unknown audio needs to remain on the no-action path. The class reporting therefore treats all three intent states as first-class outputs. Aggregate accuracy alone is insufficient because an attractive average can hide emergency misses or unknown false-action predictions.

Thresholded handling remains a boundary-rule choice rather than a tuned result in the current evaluation. A low threshold may improve emergency recall but can also make ordinary or unknown audio more likely to enter a safety-critical path. A high threshold can reduce false-action predictions but may miss urgent speech. The system does not resolve this trade-off inside the model alone. It exposes confidence to the safety-state boundary so that future boundary rules can decide how cautious to be under the current vehicle state.
