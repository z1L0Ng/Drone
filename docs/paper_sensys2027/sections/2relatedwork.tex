\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{UAV Voice Interfaces Under Rotor Noise}

Prior work has studied speech interfaces for unmanned aerial vehicles, showing that voice can be a feasible interaction channel while also highlighting that UAV platforms create a challenging acoustic environment. Kite explores automatic speech recognition for UAV control \cite{oneata19_interspeech}, and subsequent multimodal work extends UAV speech recognition beyond a single audio stream \cite{oneata2021multimodal}. These systems motivate the premise that voice can be useful around drones, but they leave open a central systems question: how should the output of a noisy voice interface be constrained before it can affect vehicle behavior?

Other UAV-focused studies explain why this question is difficult. Drone operating noise can degrade voice-command discrimination \cite{miesikowska2021uavnoise}, and speech recognition for UAV control remains sensitive to acoustic and operating context \cite{park2023uavvehicle}. Surveys on drone audition and sound-source localization similarly identify ego-noise, payload limits, and onboard real-time processing as recurring obstacles for aerial audio sensing \cite{chevtchenko2025drone_ssl}. Our work treats this platform-generated noise as the default operating environment rather than a peripheral robustness test. The goal is not only to improve recognition under noise, but to use recognition as one component of an additional UAV safety layer whose output remains mediated by a safety-state boundary.

\subsection{From Keywords to Spoken Intent Under Constraints}

Small-footprint keyword spotting is the closest mature line of on-device speech work. Compact CNNs, CRNNs, temporal-convolutional models, microcontroller-oriented keyword models, public limited-vocabulary datasets, and streaming keyword systems have shown that always-on speech interfaces must be evaluated using false accepts, misses, latency, and memory, rather than accuracy alone \cite{sainath2015cnn_kws,arik2017crnn_kws,choi2019tcresnet,warden2018speechcommands,zhang2017helloedge,rybakov2020streamingkws}. Edge-oriented speech models further demonstrate that architecture search and compact attention or convolutional designs can reduce footprint while preserving speech-recognition utility \cite{lin2018edgespeechnets,wong2020tinyspeech}. This literature is useful because it treats speech recognition as an online decision process. However, it is not the same problem as a UAV safety interaction layer: a detected keyword can be a trigger, but a nearby drone needs an intent-state update before any physical action is considered.

Speech-to-intent models provide an alternative to transcript-first pipelines when the system requires a compact action abstraction rather than a full word sequence. End-to-end spoken language understanding has shown that useful intent decisions can be learned directly from speech \cite{lugosch19_interspeech,kuo20_interspeech,mhiri20_interspeech}, including in low-latency, ASR-free, and on-device-motivated settings \cite{haghani2018audio_semantics,ijcai2021p538,le2022deliberation}. General spoken language understanding resources such as SLURP and SLUE broaden the evaluation space for speech intent tasks \cite{bastianelli-etal-2020-slurp,shon2022slue}, and multilingual benchmarks such as ML-SUPERB motivate speaker and language robustness questions \cite{shi23g_interspeech}. These benchmarks are useful, but they do not directly measure rotor self-noise, embedded deployment, or safety-state behavior.

\subsection{Robust Embedded Audio and Safety-Oriented Evaluation}

Robust speech learning and frontend techniques are relevant because the target operating condition contains severe self-noise. Multi-style training and gain control improve small-footprint keyword spotting under noisy and far-field conditions \cite{prabhavalkar2015agc}; PCEN moves part of loudness and far-field robustness into a trainable frontend \cite{wang2017pcen}; and SpecAugment improves robustness through time-frequency masking without modifying the inference model \cite{park2019specaugment}. Device mismatch and low-complexity acoustic-scene benchmarks show that audio robustness depends on microphone, enclosure, and recording conditions, not only on additive noise \cite{heittola2020dcase,schmid2024dcase}. Noisy-student training and self-supervised speech representations have further improved robustness in broader speech settings \cite{park2020improvednoisystudent,baevski2020wav2vec20,yang21c_interspeech}.

Embedded speech systems are often evaluated as standalone keyword or command recognizers, but deployment introduces constraints that are not visible from model accuracy alone. TinyML runtimes and kernels such as TensorFlow Lite Micro and CMSIS-NN make microcontroller inference practical, while MCUNet and MLPerf Tiny demonstrate that memory, operator support, latency, energy, and real-hardware measurements are first-order design variables \cite{david2021tflm,lai2018cmsisnn,lin2020mcunet,banbury2021mlperftiny}. Compression methods such as pruning, integer quantization, and distillation are also part of the deployment design space because they affect not only model size, but also operator support, activation memory, and robustness after conversion \cite{han2016deepcompression,hinton2015distilling,jacob2018integerquant}. Personalization and open-set keyword work point toward future user adaptation, but also reinforce the need to preserve rejection behavior when speech varies across users or commands \cite{leroy2019fedkws,rusci2023fewshotopenset}.

Our deployment contribution is therefore not simply an embedded model. It is the complete path from onboard audio capture to an intent-state update, which can be evaluated separately for recognition quality, runtime feasibility, response time, and safety-state behavior.

Taken together, prior work motivates the individual components of a voice-enabled UAV interface: speech input near drones, rotor-noise robustness, compact recognition, embedded inference, and existing UAV safety mechanisms. What remains is the systems question that motivates the next section: why speech should enter UAV control as a constrained intent-state mapping rather than as a transcript, keyword trigger, or direct command.
