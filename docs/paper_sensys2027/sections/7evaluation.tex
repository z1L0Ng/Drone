\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The evaluation follows the safety-layer argument of the paper. We first measure whether the acoustic recognizers separate emergency, movement, and unknown speech under the fixed rotor-noisy condition used for recognition comparison and under a compact SNR sweep. We then compare this design against transcript-first and compact speech-command alternatives. Next, we measure whether the embedded prototype can sustain onboard capture and inference at the one-second window cadence. Finally, we evaluate the safety-state behavior through containment ablations, a controlled emergency demonstration, and participant-level live recognition trials.

This organization keeps each measurement tied to the claim it can support. Recognition quality shows whether the intent-state mapping is learnable under rotor noise. Baselines test whether common speech-interface designs provide the same fit for the task. Embedded timing shows whether Akouo-Edge can produce intent-state updates locally. Safety-state checks show why unknown containment and mediated handling matter before UAV-facing action. Participant trials expose how the embedded recognizer behaves on newly collected speech from different speakers.

\subsection{Recognition Quality Under Rotor Noise}

The first evaluation isolates the recognizer from the embedded runtime and the safety-state boundary. The task has three intent states: emergency, movement, and unknown. Akouo-Ref represents the higher-quality offline recognizer used to characterize acoustic performance. Akouo-Edge represents the embedded recognizer used in the prototype. Table~\ref{tab:akouo_recognition_quality} reports the fixed -10 dB SNR comparison point for both recognizers and includes Akouo-Clean as a clean-trained diagnostic, while Table~\ref{tab:rotor_robustness} shows how Akouo-Ref changes across more severe rotor-noise mixtures.

        Akouo-Edge & 0.85 & 0.85 & 0.95 & 0.70 / 0.81 \\

At this point, Akouo-Ref reaches 86.2\% accuracy and 0.862 macro F1, with emergency precision, recall, and F1 of 0.856, 0.859, and 0.857. Akouo-Edge reaches 84.8\% accuracy and 0.85 macro F1, with emergency precision, recall, and F1 of 0.95, 0.70, and 0.81. The gap is expected: Akouo-Edge is constrained by integer inference and microcontroller runtime, while Akouo-Ref characterizes the stronger acoustic design. Akouo-Clean explains why the noisy-view recognizer is needed: it reaches 91.8\% accuracy with no added rotor noise, but drops to 42.1\% accuracy and 3.0\% emergency recall at the -10 dB comparison point.

The main trade-off is not captured by accuracy alone. Akouo-Edge preserves high emergency precision but loses emergency recall relative to Akouo-Ref. For a safety interaction layer, this means missed emergency speech is the primary embedded-recognition limitation, while false emergency or movement updates from unsupported audio must be contained by the unknown state and safety-state boundary. These tables use argmax predictions; a thresholded operating curve would still be needed to select a deployed emergency-recall versus unknown-containment operating point.

Across the -10, -15, and -20 dB mixtures, accuracy drops from 0.86 to 0.76 and unknown false-action rate rises from 0.12 to 0.23, while emergency recall remains above 0.81. This trend reinforces the boundary argument: recognizer output should remain evidence for a safety-state update, not direct action authority.

\subsection{Speech-Interface Baselines}

The second evaluation compares Akouo-Ref with two common speech-interface alternatives at the recognizer/interface level. The first is a transcript-first pipeline: a general ASR model transcribes each fixed one-second rotor-noisy clip, and a deterministic parser maps the transcript into emergency, movement, or unknown. The second is a set of compact speech-command model-family baselines trained under the same three-intent task. These baselines test different assumptions. Transcript-first parsing assumes that text is the right intermediate representation. Compact speech-command classifiers assume that a small command recognizer is enough, without the safety-state mapping and boundary semantics used by Akouo.

Table~\ref{tab:speech_interface_baselines} summarizes the comparison. The ASR row reports transcript coverage and parser keyword-hit rate because a transcript-first system can only recover an intent when usable text and a matched command word are both present. The compact model-family rows report recognition metrics under the shared task definition. These compact models are recognizer baselines, not complete safety-system baselines.

    \caption{Speech-interface baselines on fixed rotor-noisy one-second clips. The ASR row tests a transcript-first parser; compact speech-command model-family rows test recognizer alternatives under the same three-intent task.}
    \label{tab:speech_interface_baselines}
    \scriptsize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{2pt}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.31\linewidth}p{0.28\linewidth}ccc@{}}
        \toprule
        Method & Interface assumption & Acc. & Macro F1 & Emerg. R / F1 \\
        \midrule
        Akouo-Ref & Intent-state recognizer & 0.86 & 0.86 & 0.86 / 0.86 \\

        TC-ResNet8 family & Compact classifier & 0.84 & 0.84 & 0.84 / 0.86 \\

The transcript-first result supports a narrow claim. On these fixed rotor-noisy one-second clips, Whisper \texttt{tiny.en} plus the parser produces limited transcript coverage and weak emergency recall. This does not prove that all ASR systems fail near drones; it shows that a straightforward transcript-first baseline is a poor fit for the short-window safety interaction task used here.

The compact model-family comparison shows a different trade-off under the same three-intent rotor-noisy task. TC-ResNet8 is close on aggregate metrics, but it is still a recognizer-only baseline and does not provide the broader safety-layer semantics. BC-ResNet1 reaches higher emergency recall, but its recall and F1 imply lower emergency precision, producing more false emergency predictions. For a safety interaction layer, false emergency updates create unnecessary interruptions and erode trust. At this fixed comparison point, Akouo-Ref preserves the intent-state representation needed by the rest of the system while remaining competitive with compact model-family baselines.

\subsection{Embedded Inference and Intent-State Reporting}

The third evaluation measures whether the embedded intent-state source can keep up with one-second audio windows near the UAV. Figure~\ref{fig:response_time_breakdown} shows the steady-state dual-core schedule used by the prototype. Core0 is dedicated to audio capture and keeps the microphone window cadence stable. Core1 consumes the completed window, computes the log-mel frontend, runs integer inference, and reports the resulting intent-state update. This overlap is the real-time optimization in the embedded path: after the first window is filled, capture for the next window proceeds while the previous window is being recognized.

Capture occupies the one-second audio window, while core1 spends 56 ms on log-mel feature extraction and 632 ms on integer inference for the previous window. This post-capture work fits behind the next capture window, so the prototype sustains a p50/p95 update period of 1005/1005 ms over 120 delivered windows, with no drops or overruns. The first update appears after 1606 ms because the first capture must complete before recognition can begin. In the intended safety-layer deployment, the voice system starts with the UAV and remains active during operation, so the relevant operating condition is the steady-state update cadence rather than a cold first-window response. These measurements support steady-state real-time intent-state generation at the one-second window granularity; they do not claim that speech directly produces a UAV-facing action.

\subsection{Safety-State Behavior and Emergency Demonstration}

Recognition accuracy alone does not show whether a speech interface is safe to admit into UAV operation. The fourth evaluation therefore combines two forms of evidence. Offline ablations quantify how unsupported audio would leak into action pressure if recognizer outputs were bound directly to action, or if the unknown state were removed. A controlled laboratory demonstration then makes the integrated emergency-response path visible.

Table~\ref{tab:safety_ablation} summarizes the ablations. The informative quantities are the unknown false-action rate and the destination of true-unknown audio after the fallback is removed. The aggregate action-request counts are reported only as accounting context: with a balanced three-intent dataset, mapping emergency and movement to action naturally makes about two thirds of windows actionable, and removing unknown naturally drives the count to all windows.

\begin{table}[t]
    \centering
    \caption{Offline safety-state ablations focused on unknown containment. Action-pressure counts are simulated from recognizer outputs and are accounting context, not measured vehicle actuation.}
    \label{tab:safety_ablation}
    \footnotesize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.27\linewidth}p{0.66\linewidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        Ablation & Main observation \\
        \midrule
        Direct mapping & Unknown false-action rate is 0.1634; action requests are 67.57 per 100 windows under the balanced three-intent split. \\
        No unknown (Edge) & Removing unknown forces 82.25\% of true unknown windows to emergency and 17.75\% to movement. \\
        No unknown (Ref) & Removing fallback adds 32.43 action requests per 100 windows, completing the balanced split to 100 actionable windows. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The direct-mapping result shows why the safety-state boundary is not just an implementation detail. The useful observation is not that roughly two thirds of a balanced three-intent set become actionable when emergency and movement are bound to action. The useful observation is that unknown audio still leaks into action with a false-action rate of 0.1634. The no-unknown ablation makes the containment problem sharper: for the Akouo-Edge user-study predictions, true unknown audio would be forced mostly into emergency under the two-action ablation, creating unnecessary safety-critical interruptions.

    \caption{Controlled laboratory emergency-response demonstration. The panels show normal operation, nearby emergency speech while onboard inference remains active, and the resulting safe landing or stopped state through the mediated safety-state boundary.}
    \label{fig:emergency_stop_sequence}

Figure~\ref{fig:emergency_stop_sequence} shows the complementary system-level context. During the demonstration, the UAV remains in operation while Akouo-Edge continues to process onboard audio windows. When a nearby person speaks an emergency phrase, the voice layer emits an emergency intent-state update and the safety-state boundary mediates the response. The figure is included as a controlled demonstration of the interaction loop, not as complete flight-safety validation. Read together, the ablations and demonstration support the paper's safety-state argument: speech should be admitted as a mediated intent-state update, not as an immediate command.

\subsection{Participant-Level Live Recognition}

The final evaluation measures participant-level recognition robustness across different speakers, intent categories, keywords, and repeated trials using live onboard capture and inference with Akouo-Edge. The participant protocol was approved by the authors' institutional review board; identifying approval details are omitted for double-blind review. Each participant is assigned a study identifier, and participant results are reported separately before aggregation. This isolation lets the evaluation report aggregate behavior while still exposing per-participant variation, which is important for a voice interface that may fail unevenly across speakers.

Each complete participant record contains three intent categories: emergency, movement, and unknown. Each category is represented by five controlled keywords or short phrases, with ten repeated trials for each keyword, for 150 trials per complete record. Unknown trials include unsupported or irrelevant speech, and may also include silence or background audio, so the class represents audio that should not become a command rather than silence alone. Table~\ref{tab:user_study_participant_variability} reports complete and partial participant records separately, so the reader can distinguish participant-level variation from missing prompt coverage. These trials use the same onboard capture-to-inference path measured in Figure~\ref{fig:response_time_breakdown}, but they are counted as prompted utterance trials rather than as the continuous-window timing run.

The primary metrics are participant-level accuracy, emergency recall, and unknown false-action rate. Participant-level reporting is important because an average window score can hide whether the system fails for particular voices. The min--max ranges in the aggregate row make this variation explicit: emergency recall spans 0.400--0.820 across participant records, while accuracy spans 0.640--0.880. The aggregate emergency recall in Table~\ref{tab:user_study_participant_variability} is lower than the embedded test-split recall in Table~\ref{tab:akouo_recognition_quality} because the two evaluations are different domains: the test split uses open-source Speech Commands audio mixed with rotor noise, while the user study uses newly collected participant speech on the prototype recording path, introducing speaker, accent, microphone, and collection-condition mismatch.

The fixed prompt list separates participant variation from keyword variation for complete records. Partial records are still useful because they show how Akouo-Edge behaves on additional speakers, but they should be interpreted with the visible trial counts in Table~\ref{tab:user_study_participant_variability}.
